## 1. Orientation and terminology

Two research traditions use related words and should not be conflated:

| Label | Core use | Typical goals |
|--------|-----------|----------------|
| **Unterrichtsvideographie / classroom video** (for TE & instructional research) | Record real or staged lessons for analysis, measurement, reflection, PD | Professional vision, noticing, teaching quality, theory–practice integration |
| Videographie (qualitative social research; Bohnsack et al.) | Documentary / reconstructive interpretation of filmic social situations | Reconstruct *modus operandi*, frames of orientation, interaction order |

In German discourse:

- Unterrichtsvideographie - systematic audiovisual recording of teaching–learning situations (often multi-camera, with audio/context materials); research and production practice.
- Unterrichtsvideo - the artefact used in teaching (clip, whole lesson, vignette).
- Videoartefakte (Seidel 2022) - broader set including edited clips, staged vignettes, annotated modules.
- **Professionelle (Unterrichts-)Wahrnehmung / professional vision** - dominant target construct in DACH video-based teacher education research.
- **Teacher noticing** - Anglo-American twin construct (attend → interpret → often decide how to respond).

**Videography in general** in teacher training is therefore primarily the pedagogical and empirical-instructional use of classroom video, with a side strand of qualitative dokumentarische video interpretation that feeds casuistic reflection seminars.

## 2. Cross-cutting synthesis

### Cross-source observations

1. **Video is a tool, not a pedagogy.** Effectiveness depends on learning goals, task design, facilitation, material choice, and aligned assessment (Brophy 2004; Blomberg et al. 2013; Krammer & Reusser 2005; Gaudin & Chaliès 2015).
2. **Mere watching rarely produces professional learning.** Structured analysis, criteria, and facilitation are necessary (Brophy; Krammer/Reusser; Star & Strickland; van Es facilitation frameworks).
3. **Professional vision / noticing is the core target construct.** Selective attention + knowledge-based reasoning (describe / explain / predict); often focused on student thinking or classroom management quality features.
4. **Own vs. others’ video serve different functions.** Own: higher engagement, authenticity, motivation. Other: more critical distance and theory linkage. Complementary use is widely recommended (Seidel et al. 2011; Krammer 2014; Hellermann et al. 2015).
5. **Pre-service and in-service both benefit**, but designs differ: university courses often use foreign/curated clips + standardized instruments; PD often uses video clubs, own teaching, and collaborative analysis over months.
6. **Effects are strongest on proximal outcomes** (noticing quality, analysis depth, pedagogical knowledge, sometimes planning). Evidence for sustained classroom practice change and student achievement is thinner (Krammer 2012/2014; Steffensky & Kleinknecht 2016; Santagata et al. 2021; Major & Watson 2018).
7. **Infrastructure matters.** DACH portals (ViU, ProVision, unterrichtsvideos.ch, Meta-Portal unterrichtsvideos.net), TIMSS/TALIS video corpora, and FDZ Bildung secondary use have made video a shared TE resource, not only a research instrument.
8. **National teaching scripts** (TIMSS Video) and **process quality dimensions** (IPN, OECD Global Teaching InSights) supply content for comparative and quality-oriented teacher education.

### Design table (quick use)

| Design need | Frameworks | Resources |
|-------------|------------|-----------|
| What should novices see? | Professional vision, noticing | OBSERVE/Observer; IPN/TALIS quality dimensions |

## 3. Definitions and conceptual frameworks

### 3.1 Professional vision (Goodwin → teacher education)

**Goodwin, C. (1994).** Professional vision. *American Anthropologist, 96*(3), 606–633.
Type: Foundational theoretical article.
Tags: definition | framework

Summary: Defines professional vision as socially organized ways of seeing and understanding events accountable within a profession. Expertise is enacted through coding schemes, highlighting, and producing material representations (e.g. video as evidence).

**Main statements / findings:**
- Professional vision is not private perception; it is socially organized and unevenly distributed.
- Three generic practices: coding, highlighting, producing and articulating material representations.
- Seeing is shaped by discursive and material practices of a community of practice.
- Video and graphic artifacts mediate and authorize professional accounts of events.
- Theoretical root for later teacher-education work (Sherin, Seidel, DACH *professionelle Wahrnehmung*).

---

### 3.2 Teacher noticing / Learning to Notice

**van Es, E. A., & Sherin, M. G. (2002).** Learning to notice: Scaffolding new teachers’ interpretations of classroom interactions. *Journal of Technology and Teacher Education, 10*(4), 571–596.
Type: Framework + empirical article.
Tags: definition | framework | method | preservice

Summary: Introduces the influential *Learning to Notice* framework. Reform teaching requires attending to students’ ideas in the moment; novices struggle. Multimedia/video scaffolds selective attention and interpretation.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Noticing comprises: identifying noteworthy events; using knowledge to reason about interactions; connecting events to broader teaching/learning principles.
- Video tools support noticing without the pressure of live classroom response.
- Novices need structured support to move beyond surface classroom features.
- Framework becomes the conceptual base for later video-club research.

**Jacobs, V. R., Lamb, L. L. C., & Philipp, R. A. (2010).** Professional noticing of children’s mathematical thinking. *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, 41*(2), 169–202.
Type: Framework + large-sample empirical.
Tags: definition | framework | method | preservice | inservice

Summary: Defines professional noticing of children’s mathematical thinking as attending to strategies, interpreting understanding, and deciding how to respond. Studies 131 pre- and in-service elementary teachers.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Three interrelated skills: attend, interpret, decide how to respond.
- Expertise (including specialized PD) is associated with more sophisticated noticing.
- Guided video and student-work questions with rubrics can measure noticing.
- Highly influential operationalization for later video-based noticing interventions.

**Sherin, M. G., Jacobs, V. R., & Philipp, R. A. (Eds.). (2011).** *Mathematics teacher noticing: Seeing through teachers’ eyes.* Routledge.
Type: Edited handbook-style volume.
Tags: definition | framework | method

Summary: Book-length treatment of mathematics teacher noticing with cognitive, sociocultural, and anthropological perspectives; video is discussed as a research instrument and PD medium.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Teacher noticing is a central construct for research on teaching expertise and teacher learning across multiple theoretical traditions.
- Core components typically include attending, interpreting, and (in some models) deciding how to respond.
- Video is both a research instrument and a professional-development medium for studying and developing noticing.
- Expertise differences appear systematically in what is noticed and how classroom events are interpreted.
- The volume consolidates the field that later video-club and professional-vision programs operationalize.

### 3.3 Professionelle Unterrichtswahrnehmung / knowledge-based reasoning (DACH)

**Seidel, T., & Stürmer, K. (2014).** Modeling and measuring the structure of professional vision in preservice teachers. *American Educational Research Journal, 51*(4), 739–771.
https://doi.org/10.3102/0002831214531321
Type: Measurement / model validation (OBSERVE lineage).
Tags: definition | method | preservice | effects | project

Summary: Models professional vision for pre-service teachers and validates video-based measurement (Observer instrument family). Central for German operationalizations of *professionelle Wahrnehmung*.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Professional vision = noticing + knowledge-based reasoning (typically describe, explain, predict).
- Video-based items can reliably capture differences among pre-service teachers.
- Declarative knowledge and professional vision are related but not identical.
- Instrument widely used as outcome measure for video seminars in Germany.
- Ties reasoning to teaching-quality facets (e.g. goal clarity, learning support, learning climate).

**Seidel, T. (2022).** Professionelle Unterrichtswahrnehmung als Teil von Expertise im Lehrberuf. Weiterentwicklungsperspektiven für die videobasierte Lehrerforschung. In R. Junker et al. (Eds.), *Lehren und Forschen mit Videos in der Lehrkräftebildung* (pp. 17–36). Waxmann.
https://doi.org/10.31244/9783830995111
Type: Handbook chapter (OA edited volume).
Tags: definition | framework | preservice | inservice | effects

Summary: Positions professional classroom vision as a component of teacher expertise within a career-phase model. Introduces Videoartefakte (not only raw classroom recordings: clips, staged vignettes, annotated formats) and argues for biography-sensitive video pedagogy across the teaching career.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Professional vision = noticing (knowledge-guided attention) + reasoning (describe / explain / predict; Seidel & Stürmer, 2014).
- Videos support practice-based knowledge by decomposing complex teaching into learnable representations.
- Publication volume on video-based teacher education has risen sharply; approaches remain heterogeneous.
- Need for an expertise-development framework spanning novices → intermediates → experts rather than phase-isolated studies.
- Video alone is not a didactic “self-runner”; goals, instructional approach, video type, limits/context info, and assessment must be aligned.
- Videoartefakte expand the material base beyond authentic full lessons to designed vignettes and annotated modules.

**Seidel, T., Blomberg, G., & Stürmer, K. (2010).** „Observer“ – Validierung eines videobasierten Instruments zur Erfassung der professionellen Wahrnehmung von Unterricht. In Kompetenzmodellierung (ZfPäd Beiheft 56), 296–306. (pedocs)
Type: Project/instrument chapter.
Tags: method | project | framework

Summary: Documents the Observer tool as a video-based diagnostic of pre-service professional vision with expert-referenced scoring of knowledge-based reasoning.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Professional vision can be assessed with standardized classroom video clips and expert-referenced rating items delivered via an online platform.
- The instrument targets pedagogical-psychological competence facets of observing teaching (e.g. goal orientation, learning support, learning climate).
- Expert ratings provide reference standards against which pre-service judgments are scored.
- Observer functions as both research measure and instructional tool for teacher education courses.
- Validation work underpins the later Seidel & Stürmer (2014) structural model of professional vision.

### 3.4 Unterrichtsvideos as medium (Swiss–German foundation)

**Krammer, K., & Reusser, K. (2005).** Unterrichtsvideos als Medium der Aus- und Weiterbildung von Lehrpersonen. *Beiträge zur Lehrerbildung, 23*(1), 35–50.
https://doi.org/10.25656/01:13561 · [peDOCS OA](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2017/13561/pdf/BZL_2005_1_35_50.pdf)
Type: Conceptual overview.
Tags: definition | method | preservice | inservice | framework

Summary: Positions classroom videos as a medium-specific resource for pre- and in-service education: high multimodal density, process authenticity, and replay without action pressure. Contrasts microteaching/imitation with case-based reflective analysis.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Media-specific advantages: authenticity, information density, re-viewability without action pressure.
- Core potentials: reflection on complex processes; expansion of subjective theories; flexible thinking about alternatives; theory–practice linking; shared professional language; multi-perspective integration.
- Two basic functions: (1) show/illustrate desired practices; (2) reflect/analyze quality of teaching–learning processes.
- Limits: camera as selective frame; no interaction with protagonists; mere watching rarely produces insight (citing Brophy).
- Design dimensions: format, content (own/other; authentic/staged; whole/clip), learning setting, goal (imitation vs analysis).
- Shift from behaviorist microteaching to case-based, inquiry-oriented reflection (Schön; action research).

### 3.5 Case-based learning with classroom video

**Krammer, K. (2014).** Fallbasiertes Lernen mit Unterrichtsvideos in der Lehrerinnen- und Lehrerbildung. *Beiträge zur Lehrerinnen- und Lehrerbildung, 32*(2), 164–175.
https://doi.org/10.25656/01:13863 · [peDOCS OA](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2017/13863/pdf/BZL_2014_2_164_175.pdf)
Type: Research synthesis.
Tags: definition | method | preservice | inservice | effects | framework

Summary: German-language synthesis of case-based learning with classroom videos: theory (situated learning, reflective practice, professional vision), practice formats (own/other; video clubs; PSC), empirical effects, and efficacy conditions.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Videographed lessons are cases of professional action; re-viewing supports multi-aspect analysis without immediate action pressure.
- Video-supported reflection is more differentiated/focused than non-video reflection.
- Own videos: more authentic/motivating; other videos: better critical distance.
- Effects reported for analysis competence, attention to student thinking, and (some) classroom management knowledge; transfer to student achievement sparsely documented.
- Efficacy conditions: fit of video + focus + setting + assessment; guided questions; facilitation; time; accompanying materials.
- Often recommended path: start with other videos (discourse norms + focus), then own videos.
- Videos are tools; form of use decides impact (Brophy 2004).

### 3.6 Lesson Analysis Framework (Santagata)

**Santagata, R. (2014).** Unterrichtsvideos in der Lehrerinnen- und Lehrerbildung: Zentrale Fragestellungen, Instrumente und Einschätzungen für Forschung und Praxis. *Beiträge zur Lehrerinnen- und Lehrerbildung, 32*(2), 196–209.
https://doi.org/10.25656/01:13865 · [peDOCS OA](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2017/13865/pdf/BZL_2014_2_196_209.pdf)
Type: Design model (German for DACH audience).
Tags: framework | preservice | method | effects

Summary: Four-component model for video in teacher learning: (1) purpose, (2) video types, (3) viewing modalities, (4) assessment. Illustrated with LLMT project and Lesson Analysis Framework.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Purpose drives all subsequent decisions (PCK, reform practices, noticing, equity…).
- Type decisions: whole lesson vs clip; own vs other; expert vs novice models; exemplary vs ordinary teaching.
- Own teaching: higher engagement; other teaching: better stimulus for critical analysis (Seidel et al.).
- Without guidance, pre-service teachers focus on superficial aspects (clothing, tone of voice).
- Structured frameworks (LAF: goals → student progress evidence → effective strategies → alternatives) outperform unstructured viewing.
- Instruments: Observer (Seidel); Classroom Video Analysis / CVA (Kersting) for quantitative measurement.
- LLMT study: video-enhanced group outperformed control on attending to student thinking and proposing instructional improvements.
- Systematic use needed so evidence can improve designs iteratively; culture of trust essential.

**Santagata, R., & Angelici, G. (2010).** Studying the impact of the Lesson Analysis Framework on preservice teachers’ abilities to reflect on videos of classroom teaching. *Journal of Teacher Education, 61*(4), 339–349.
Type: Empirical.
Tags: framework | preservice | effects

Summary: Framework-supported analysis improved pre-service reflection on video relative to less structured approaches.

**Main statements / findings:**
- The Lesson Analysis Framework structures video reflection around lesson goals, student learning evidence, instructional strategies, and alternatives.
- Pre-service teachers using the framework produced more sophisticated analyses of classroom video than peers with less structured guidance.
- Structured scaffolds outperform open-ended “watch and comment” tasks for developing reflection quality.
- Provides experimental support for Santagata’s design model later published for the DACH audience (2014).
- Supports treating analysis ability as a teachable outcome of guided video work in methods courses.

### 3.7 Approximation of practice (Grossman)

**Grossman, P., Compton, C., Igra, D., Ronfeldt, M., Shahan, E., & Williamson, P. (2009).** Teaching practice: A cross-professional perspective. *Teachers College Record, 111*(9), 2055–2100.
Also: Grossman, P., Hammerness, K., & McDonald, M. (2009). Redefining teaching, re-imagining teacher education. *Teachers and Teaching, 15*(2), 273–289.
Type: Conceptual framework.
Tags: framework

Summary: Cross-professional analysis of how professions prepare novices; introduces representations, decompositions, and approximations of practice.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Three pedagogies of practice: representations of practice (e.g. classroom video, cases); decomposition of practice; approximations of practice (rehearsals, simulations, coached components).
- Video often serves as representation and as stimulus for approximations (e.g. plan a next move after a clip).
- Positions video not only as reflection medium but as infrastructure for practice-based teacher education.

### 3.8 Qualitative Videographie (Bohnsack / documentary method)

**Bohnsack, R. (2009/2011).** *Qualitative Bild- und Videointerpretation. Die dokumentarische Methode.* Opladen: Budrich / utb.
Type: Methodological monograph.
Tags: definition | method

Summary: Documentary method tradition (Mannheim, Panofsky, Imdahl) for qualitative image and video interpretation. Reconstructs collective knowledge and *modus operandi*, not primarily “trains teachers with lesson clips.”

**Main statements / findings:**
- Video as material for reconstructive analysis of practice knowledge (communicative vs. conjunctive knowledge).
- Differs from psychometric/process–product coding of teaching quality.
- Methodologically adjacent but not identical to professional-vision training with classroom video.
- Feeds German casuistic / documentary classroom research in TE (see Asbrand & Martens 2018).

**Asbrand, B., & Martens, M. (2018).** *Dokumentarische Unterrichtsforschung.* Springer VS.
Type: Textbook (qualitative methodology).
Tags: definition | method | framework

Summary: Systematic textbook for documentary classroom research using video/audio; reconstructive analysis of practice knowledge of teachers and students.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Video is primary data for reconstructing implicit practice knowledge of teachers and students (documentary method).
- Differs from psychometric/process–product video coding (IPN-type): aims at typification of orientations, not scoring “good teaching” indicators alone.
- Provides detailed guidance on recording, sequence selection, and multimodal interpretation for qualitative classroom research.
- Frequently referenced in German teacher-education contexts that use video for casuistic/documentary reflection rather than professional-vision tests.
- Clarifies the methodological branch of Videographie that coexists with instructional-research uses of Unterrichtsvideos.

### 3.9 Five research-based heuristics

**Blomberg, G., Renkl, A., Sherin, M. G., Borko, H., & Seidel, T. (2013).** Five research-based heuristics for using video in pre-service teacher education. *Journal for Educational Research Online, 5*(1), 90–114.
https://doi.org/10.25656/01:8021 · [peDOCS OA](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2013/8021/pdf/JERO_2013_1_Blomberg_et_al_Five_research_based_heuristics.pdf)
Type: Research synthesis / design heuristics.
Tags: framework | method | preservice

Summary: DE-US synthesis deriving five heuristics for when, how, and why to use video with pre-service teachers.

**Main statements / findings:**
1. Identify specific learning goals suited to video (e.g. noticing student thinking).
2. Embed video in appropriate instructional strategies (video is not neutral).
3. Choose video material deliberately (own vs others; exemplary vs typical; clip length).
4. Explicitly address strengths and limitations of video as a medium.
5. Align assessment with instruction and goals.
- Video affordances: manageable chunks, multi-perspective re-viewing, “window” without action pressure, high motivation - but also risk of unfocused noticing.
- Controlled experimental evidence was judged relatively thin; design quality explains mixed findings.
- Heuristics also transfer usefully to in-service PD.

## 4. Methods of classroom video work

### 4.1 Recording, ethics, selection

**Derry, S. J. (Ed.). (2007).** *Guidelines for video research in education: Recommendations from an expert panel.* Data Research and Development Center (NORC / University of Chicago) / NSF-IERI.
[Open PDF](https://cpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/faculty.sites.uci.edu/dist/2/425/files/2011/03/video-research-guidelines.pdf)
Type: Methodological handbook / expert guidelines.
Tags: method

Summary: Consensus guidelines on recording strategies, clip selection, analysis, sharing, learning-from-video design, and ethics (with sample consent forms).

**Main statements / findings:**
- Treat recording as a sampling problem; continuous recording preferred over on/off chopping that forecloses later analyses.
- Two-camera setups often recommended: fixed wide + mobile follow; prioritize audio (external mics).
- Selection stages depend on purpose: video-as-data, narrative power, PD cases.
- Analysis practices: play-by-play; coding/counting; progressive hypothesis refinement.
- Ethics: informed consent for collection and secondary use/sharing; special care for minors and archival sharing.
- Video-based PD is treated as its own research domain (learning with video).

**Kramer, C., Spicker, S., & Kaspar, K. (2023).** *Manual zur Erstellung von Unterrichtsvideographien.* Universität zu Köln / ZuS (Qualitätsoffensive Lehrerbildung).
[Project](https://zus.uni-koeln.de/produkte-und-konzepte/manual-zur-erstellung-von-unterrichtsvideographie)
Type: Practical methodological manual (German).
Tags: method | preservice | project

Summary: German-language production manual for classroom videography in teacher education: pre-production, production, post-production, with phase checklists (ViLLA / ZuS ecosystem).

**Main statements / findings:**
- Videography can capture simultaneous multi-actor events (position, voice, facial expression, gesture, posture) for later analysis.
- Phases: Vorbereitung → Produktion → Nachbereitung (secure storage, editing, provision).
- Technical quality and ethical consent are prerequisites for usable teaching materials.
- Links production standards to analysis of classroom management and professional vision.

### 4.2 Instructional sequencing (rule–example vs example–rule)

**Seidel, T., Blomberg, G., & Renkl, A. (2013).** Instructional strategies for using video in teacher education. *Teaching and Teacher Education, 34*, 56–65.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tate.2013.03.004
Type: Quasi-experiment (≈54 German pre-service teachers).
Tags: method | preservice | effects | framework

Summary: Compares rule–example (principles first, then clips) vs example–rule (clips first, then derive principles) over a three-month module.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Rule–example > example–rule on factual knowledge (reported d ≈ 1.86) and Observer-based evaluation (d ≈ 0.55).
- Example–rule > rule–example on lesson-planning challenges identified (d ≈ 0.91), especially situational challenges (d ≈ 2.40).
- Strategy choice depends on whether knowledge acquisition or transfer/planning is primary.
- Limitations: no pure non-video control; early-stage sample; no long-term classroom transfer measured.

### 4.3 Facilitation of video analysis

**van Es, E. A., Tunney, J., Goldsmith, L. T., & Seago, N. (2014).** A framework for the facilitation of teachers’ analysis of video. *Journal of Teacher Education, 65*(4), 340–356.
https://doi.org/10.1177/0022487114534266
Type: Empirical framework (facilitation moves).
Tags: method | framework

Summary: Analyzes in-the-moment facilitator moves in two video-based PD programs; offers a facilitation framework.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Productive video analysis is not automatic; facilitation is a distinct professional practice.
- Core practices (as commonly cited): orient the group to the task; sustain an inquiry stance; maintain focus on the video and student thinking; support group collaboration.
- Framework widely used to train facilitators of video clubs and lesson study with video.
- Access: Full text often paywalled; structure confirmed via secondary scholarly summaries.

### 4.4 Own vs. others’ video

**Seidel, T., Stürmer, K., Blomberg, G., Kobarg, M., & Schwindt, K. (2011).** Teacher learning from analysis of videotaped classroom situations: Does it make a difference whether teachers observe their own teaching or that of others? *Teaching and Teacher Education, 27*(2), 259–267.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tate.2010.08.009
Type: Experimental comparison.
Tags: effects | method | preservice | inservice

Summary: Compares own and other classroom video for knowledge activation and professional vision.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Own video: higher engagement and motivation; somewhat better identification of teaching–learning components.
- Other video: better critical analysis stance.
- Both types useful; complementarity recommended in DACH training designs.
- Experience with video moderates effects.

**Hellermann, C., Gold, B., & Holodynski, M. (2015).** Die Wirkung der Analyse eigener und fremder Unterrichtsvideos auf die professionelle Wahrnehmung von Klassenführung. *Zeitschrift für Entwicklungspsychologie und Pädagogische Psychologie, 47*(2), 97–109.
Type: Quasi-experiment (classroom management).
Tags: effects | preservice | method

Summary: Own vs. other (vs. combined) video analysis for classroom-management professional vision in pre-service teachers.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Both conditions showed learning gains in professional vision of classroom management.
- Differentiates motivational vs. analytical contributions of own-video analysis within a controlled design.
- Complements Seidel et al. (2011) with a CM-specific German sample.
- Access: Hogrefe paywall; abstract/citations used.

### 4.5 Research videography of teaching quality (IPN)

**Seidel, T., Prenzel, M., Rimmele, R., Dalehefte, I. M., Herweg, C., Kobarg, M., & Schwindt, K. (2006).** Blicke auf den Physikunterricht. Ergebnisse der IPN Videostudie. *Zeitschrift für Pädagogik, 52*(6), 799–821.
https://doi.org/10.25656/01:4489 · [peDOCS OA](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2011/4489/pdf/ZfPaed_2006_Seidel_Prenzel_Rimmele_Blicke_Physikunterricht_D_A.pdf)
Type: Empirical large-scale video study.
Tags: method | project | effects | framework

Summary: German research video study of secondary physics teaching (IPN Kiel; DFG BiQua). Exemplifies Unterrichtsvideographie as research method: multi-perspective coding of classroom organization, goal orientation, learning support, error culture, experimentation, linked to student outcomes.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Video enables process-level description and explanation of teaching–learning beyond self-report.
- High goal clarity/transparency positively related to cognitive learning aspects; experimental work also linked to cognitive outcomes.
- Learning-support features more strongly linked to attitudes/interest in physics than to pure cognitive gains.
- Established coding standards later reused in teacher-education video material and instruments (LUV, OBSERVE lineage).
- Made observable process quality a central object of German empirical instructional research (parallel to TIMSS video).

### 4.6 Trends in German video-based research

**Seidel, T., & Thiel, F. (2017).** Standards und Trends der videobasierten Lehr-Lernforschung. *Zeitschrift für Erziehungswissenschaft, 20*(Suppl. 1), 1–21.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s11618-017-0726-6
Type: Special-issue intro / trends.
Tags: method | framework | effects

Summary: Editorial synthesis identifying three trends: (1) process-oriented capture of teaching–learning via videography; (2) video for measuring and fostering professional competencies (esp. professional vision); (3) digital infrastructures and new video formats.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Videography advanced from large research studies to routine teacher-education use.
- Need for methodological standards (coding, reliability, ecological validity).
- Growing use of staged/scripted vignettes alongside authentic lessons.
- Assessment instruments (video-based tests) become a parallel research line to instructional design.

## 5. Uses in pre-service teacher education

### 5.1 Swiss Berufsstudien formats (PHZ Luzern)

### 6.4 State of the field: in-service video TPD

## 7. Effects and outcomes

### 7.1 Major international reviews

**Gaudin, C., & Chaliès, S. (2015).** Video viewing in teacher education and professional development: A literature review. *Educational Research Review, 16*, 41–67.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2015.06.001
Type: Literature review (approximately 255 studies).
Tags: review | framework | effects | preservice | inservice

Summary: Maps video viewing in initial teacher education and PD by activity structure, objectives, video types, and reported effects.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Video viewing is widely used but heterogeneous in design and purpose.
- Objectives commonly include developing professional vision, reflection, knowledge, and classroom practice.
- Two core activity types while viewing: selective attention and knowledge-based reasoning.
- Types of video (own teaching, peer, exemplary, authentic classroom) afford different learning processes.
- Effects generally positive on noticing/analysis; evidence on instructional change and student outcomes more limited and uneven.
- Facilitation, task structure, and institutional context strongly moderate outcomes.
- Access: Often paywalled; findings rest on abstract + consistent secondary citation.

**Brophy, J. (Ed.). (2004).** *Using video in teacher education* (Advances in Research on Teaching, Vol. 10). Elsevier.
Type: Edited handbook volume.
Tags: framework | method | preservice | inservice | definition

Summary: Foundational collection consolidating early-2000s theory and practice. Brophy’s framing: video is a delivery technology for content and experience, not content itself.

**Main statements / findings:**
- Productive uses include case methods, analysis of teaching, reflection, and modeling practice.
- Design of tasks, facilitation, and selection of clips determines learning value.
- Field moving from microteaching-era uses toward analysis of authentic classroom complexity.
- “Simply watching” classroom videos usually does not yield many new insights (widely cited claim).

### 7.3 Mathematics noticing programs (systematic review)

**Santagata, R., König, J., Scheiner, T., Nguyen, H., Adleff, A.-K., Yang, X., & Kaiser, G. (2021).** Mathematics teacher learning to notice: A systematic review of studies of video-based programs. *ZDM – Mathematics Education, 53*(1), 119–134.
https://doi.org/10.1007/s11858-020-01216-z
Type: Systematic review (35 intervention studies).
Tags: review | effects | framework | method | preservice | inservice

Summary: Two-decade systematic review of video-based programs designed to develop mathematics teachers’ noticing.

**Main statements / findings:**
- 25 pre-service, 8 in-service, 2 mixed; vast majority cognitive-psychological framing.
- Noticing facets targeted: attending/perceiving (≈89%), interpreting/reasoning (≈80%), responding/decision-making (only ≈37%).
- Focus overwhelmingly on student thinking (≈83%); equity almost absent.
- Author-reported improvements: attending ≈83%, reasoning ≈66%, responding ≈23%; change in classroom practice only ≈17%.
- Few studies used specialized video software, grouping designs, or randomization.
- Calls for socio-cultural/equity-oriented noticing; response facets; practice outcomes; stronger research designs.

### 7.4 Bridge to student learning (usable knowledge)

**Kersting, N. B., Givvin, K. B., Thompson, B. J., Santagata, R., & Stigler, J. W. (2012).** Measuring usable knowledge: Teachers’ analyses of mathematics classroom videos predict teaching quality and student learning. *American Educational Research Journal, 49*(3), 568–589.
Type: Empirical (rare student-outcome link).
Tags: effects | method | inservice | framework

Summary: Assesses “usable” teaching knowledge via teachers’ written analyses of classroom video clips on fractions. Video-analysis scores predict instructional quality and student learning.

**Main statements / findings:**

## 10. Limitations, critiques, and research gaps

### Limitations repeatedly noted in the literature

1. **Video is not self-sufficient.** Without goals, scaffolds, and facilitation, viewing can reinforce existing beliefs.
2. **Thin causal evidence** for transfer to classroom practice and student learning; many small qualitative/case studies; few RCTs with non-video controls.
3. **Construct narrowness.** Cognitive-psychological “attending + interpreting” dominates; responding/decision-making, equity-oriented noticing, and socio-cultural professional vision under-studied (Santagata et al. 2021).
4. **In-service under-researched** relative to pre-service (Major & Watson; Santagata et al.).
5. **Reporting opacity.** Many papers under-describe clip length, selection criteria, facilitator moves, dosage, and fidelity.
6. **Ethical and practical burdens.** Consent, data protection (esp. DSGVO), storage, production logistics; risk of performative “teaching for the camera.”
7. **Ecological validity trade-offs.** Short curated clips enable focus but may strip complexity; full lessons preserve complexity but overload novices.
8. **Commercial overclaim risk.** Vendor evaluations of video coaching sometimes outpace independent peer review.

### Open research questions

- Under what conditions does improved noticing transfer into in-the-moment instructional decisions and measurable student learning?
- Optimal mixes of own vs. others’ video across career stages (novice → expert).
- Comparative effectiveness of facilitation models (video club vs coaching vs self-guided platform) at scale.
- Equity and socio-cultural noticing (power, language, inclusion) - not only “student mathematical thinking.”
- Role of new media such as 360-degree video, virtual-reality classrooms, multi-perspective capture, and automated coding relative to conventional 2D classroom video.
- Longitudinal designs linking university video modules → Referendariat/induction → in-service practice.
- Cost-effectiveness and institutional conditions for sustaining video PD beyond funded projects.
- Pre-registered experiments and shared coding schemes for true meta-analysis of effects.
